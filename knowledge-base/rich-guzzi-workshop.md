# Rich Guzzi Workshop Notes (2025)

Notes from Rich Guzzi's live multi-day workshop last year, with guest sessions from Ashley (LinkedIn prospecting and branding) and Joe (Facebook ads). The recording quality was poor, so some numbers below are best readings of garbled speech.

- **Part 1** covers in-person selling: phone checkout links, digital delivery, and the audio product ladder. Its raw transcript is in [transcripts/rich-guzzi-2025.md](transcripts/rich-guzzi-2025.md).
- **Parts 2 and 3** cover domains, websites, the CRM funnel, social media, LinkedIn, stage craft, media appearances, branding, and the nine-step free speech with an offer. Their raw transcript is not saved in the repo.

Tags: **[Primary]** for what the presenters taught, **[Self-reported]** for their claimed results.

# Part 1: In-person selling

## The visibility stack

Guzzi draws three layers: the website on top, the CRM in the middle, and you at the bottom. When someone looks you up, they should find the website right away, and the CRM handles everything after that. He treats the CRM as the control center. [Primary]

He also runs a mock radio show and on-camera practice during the workshop. Students dress for camera on day two and record again on the last day so they can compare. [Primary]

## Be a walking lead machine

- People buy your promise to solve their problem more than they buy the product. [Primary]
- Successful buyers decide fast and don't change their minds. Spot them and give them a way to act on the spot. [Primary]
- Sell everywhere: the supermarket, a bookstore's self-help aisle, after a show. He calls this "hand-to-hand combat." [Primary]

## One checkout link per offer, saved on your phone

1. Build a checkout form in the CRM for every product. Some products get two: one for pay-in-full and one for a payment plan. His example: $1,000 in full, or $250 now with $250 billed automatically each month for three months. [Primary]
2. Copy each form's unique link into a single master note on your phone. Guzzi says he has about 100. [Self-reported]
3. When a conversation turns to an offer, open the note, tap the link, and text it to the person with a line about what you discussed. ("Hey Wayne, here's the hypnosis class we were talking about. Let me know if you have questions.") [Primary]
4. Your text thread becomes your to-do list. When the CRM texts you that someone paid, swipe them off the list. Follow up with everyone else. [Primary]

When you meet someone, ask for their number and type it into a new text yourself. Write the message as a note to them that also reminds you of the conversation. ("Great meeting you. Let's talk next week, I have the perfect plan for you.") [Primary]

## Let the system bill payment plans

Automatic billing keeps every buyer on the same schedule. Manual billing slips by a week, buyers lose momentum, and some stop paying. Guzzi says each person has a dollar number in their head; once the balance owed passes it, they quit paying. Keep credit short and don't push buyers into financing terms you wouldn't sign yourself. [Primary]

## Ladder your events

Sell the next date while the current one fills. He had 15 tickets sold for an August 5 to 10 event and a second funnel already running for the next date, so a sold-out event rolls straight into the next. [Primary, Self-reported]

## The digital delivery app

Guzzi paid a developer (he estimates about $50,000) to build a phone app holding his whole audio library. He picks a program, taps checkout, enters the buyer's phone number, and the program lands on their phone. He takes the money on the spot. No inventory, shipping, or printing. The app also adds the buyer to his CRM. [Self-reported]

He built it after a cruise-ship show where buyers left a 400-person product line and later asked him for programs on the pool deck. [Self-reported]

The same app can send an electronic press kit. After a show, a real estate broker asked him to speak to 50 agents. Guzzi texted a confirmation plus the press kit for that talk. The broker watched it in the parking lot and expected Guzzi's call the next day. Guzzi calls this "spin-off business": deals that come from work you're already doing. [Self-reported]

## Product ladder for an audio library

Record 15 programs to start. [Primary]

| Tier | Example | Price |
|---|---|---|
| Free lead magnet | One program to give away | $0 |
| Low-cost entry | A sleep program | about $5 |
| Single programs | Stop smoking, weight loss | $20 each |
| Bundle | 12 programs | $100 ("the sweet spot") |

Sell the high-demand program, like stop smoking, on its own as well. Buyers who want it often won't look for it inside a bundle. [Primary]

Build two stacks. The general stack covers the problems that bring in about 95% of clients, and Guzzi suggests copying his. The niche stack has 12 programs for your specialty, such as peak performance for executives or speakers. [Primary]

## Recording the programs

- **Simple way:** a handheld digital recorder from a music store, recorded inside a clothes closet to deaden sound. [Primary]
- **Pro way:** a good microphone into recording software on a laptop, still in a quiet room or closet. [Primary]
- Record the induction once, then a daytime outro and a nighttime outro, then all the middle sections. An engineer splices each middle between an induction and an outro, adds music, fades, and removes clicks. [Primary]
- His music starts at 80 beats per minute, the average resting heart rate, and slows to about 60 so the listener's heart rate follows. He also offers versions at solfeggio frequencies such as 417 Hz. [Primary]
- He uses AI to draft scripts and says he has about 1,000. [Self-reported]

## Scoring: seven is perfect

Guzzi scores performances out of ten, with seven as a perfect score. The top three points are reserved for nights when everything lines up. [Primary]

He sorts shows into four levels by how the client reacts afterward. A poor show ends with a haggle over pay. After a good show the client is friendly. A great show gets a "let's do this again." An excellent show has the client booking the next date before you leave. [Primary]

A pro keeps working until they get it right. A master keeps working until they can't get it wrong. [Primary]

# Part 2: Domains, websites, and the CRM

## Domains are your land

- Buy a domain people can remember after hearing it once on air. Guzzi plugged a joke domain on a Tampa radio show and says fans still quote it 20 years later. [Self-reported]
- He owns a "[city]tickets.com" domain for towns he plays and keeps about 1,000 domains in reserve, with 10 to 15 live sites at a time. He repoints a spare domain whenever a new event needs one. [Self-reported]
- A taken domain may still be for sale. He bought hypnosisworld.com for $2,500 against a $14,000 asking price by reading the seller as recognition-driven and letting the seller feel they won. [Self-reported]

## The website is the building

- Put the lead magnet at the top of the home page, like a warehouse store's cheap headline item that pulls people in. [Primary]
- A one-page site that works beats a 50-page brochure. Every page should lead to a checkout or a contact form. [Primary]
- Use CRM forms for bookings. A booking form should collect the date and budget, alert you, and start a follow-up list. [Primary]
- Automate one-on-one sessions end to end: calendar booking, payment, intake form, and a pre-session audio the client listens to before arriving. [Primary]
- For high-ticket offers, sell a strategy call (his example: $200) and credit it toward the purchase. Stop offering free calls once you're busy. [Primary]
- Make the site editable from your phone with drag-and-drop. Waiting weeks for a web designer to post an event costs you promotion time. [Primary]
- Set ticket caps per event. He sells slightly past room size (37 tickets for about 30 seats) to cover no-shows. [Primary]

## Run your own ticketing and payments

- Third-party ticketing fees come out of what the buyer would have paid you. Guzzi built his own ticketing into his sites. [Primary]
- He takes online payments through Stripe and in-person payments through Square, at about 3% each. He says Stripe sides with high-volume sellers on chargebacks and PayPal sides with buyers. [Primary]
- "Four-walling" means renting a room, such as a hotel ballroom, and selling the tickets yourself. [Primary]

## Hosting and platforms

- Use WordPress with a drag-and-drop editor. Guzzi says Wix sites rank poorly in Google search. Use GoDaddy for domains only, not hosting. [Primary]
- Entry hosting runs about $200 a year. Move to a dedicated server (about $1,000 to $1,500 a year) once traffic spikes, and to cloud hosting such as AWS when big shows crash servers. [Primary]
- Avoid platforms that are hard to leave, such as HubSpot and Kajabi, and those that charge per funnel. He used Keap (formerly Infusionsoft) and was building his own CRM. [Primary]
- He connected Twilio to Keap for texting and says that cut his cost from 4 cents a text to $7 per 1,000. [Self-reported]

## Profile buyers on the intake form

Guzzi sorts buyers into four motivations: self-preservation (safety and security), money, relationships, and recognition. He hides one question in the intake form after an ordinary one:

1. "What are you hoping to gain from the session?"
2. "What is most important to you?" with options for safety and security, making more money, better relationships, and getting the recognition you deserve.

Sell to the answer. A recognition buyer wants to hear their result will impress people; a self-preservation buyer wants safety. Follow-up questions can find their learning style. [Primary]

## Build the funnel in tagged steps

Guzzi splits the funnel into separate CRM groups, each set by a tag, so he can see where people drop off: [Primary]

1. Requested the lead magnet
2. Downloaded the lead magnet (tracked separately, because many requesters never download)
3. Bought the tripwire
4. Bought the two-hour seminar
5. Completed the seminar

Rules he teaches:

- **Deliver the lead magnet up to five times**, one email a day with different subject lines. The sequence stops as soon as they click the download link. [Primary]
- **Upsell with five emails, then three harder ones.** Pair each email with a text. [Primary]
- **Never throw away non-buyers.** Everyone who runs out of emails moves to long-term nurture. [Primary]
- **Keep sales, onboarding, and completion as separate automations.** Moving buyers out of the sales sequence frees it for the next launch, so you reuse one funnel every month instead of rebuilding it. [Primary]
- **Use three onboarding templates:** digital delivery, live online training, and live in-person event. [Primary]
- **Write event reminders backward from the event date** (three months, two months, one month, three weeks, two weeks, one week, the day). Send every buyer a master welcome email first, because one sequence can't mix delay timers and date timers. For the next run, change only the dates. [Primary]
- **Completion triggers the next upsell.** Lead magnet, tripwire, core offer, high-ticket offer (sold through a strategy call), then the elite mastery program. [Primary]
- **Use a gift tag** to drop someone into a program manually without a purchase. [Primary]
- **Duplicate templates before editing them.** [Primary]
- **Fix one variable at a time.** If email three loses people, change one element of email two (subject, image, headline) and retest. [Primary]
- **Text the hot clickers yourself.** The CRM shows who clicked a sales email; follow up with them by hand. [Primary]

If the tripwire is too much work to build, skip it and send lead magnet downloads straight to the two-hour seminar. [Primary]

## The elite program: rotating modules

Teach one module per month on a 12-month loop so a buyer can join in any month. His stage-hypnosis example rotates pre-talk, inductions, comedy writing, working with comics, emerging subjects, and safety after the show. [Primary]

## Long-term nurture

After a sequence ends, people get 10 to 12 weeks of nurture emails on the topic they opted into, every few days at first and weekly later. Then they move to a master mailing list that runs until they unsubscribe. [Primary]

Send content, not just pitches, and put the offer in a P.S. Guzzi runs long videos through Opus Clip to cut short social clips. [Primary]

## Sell to your existing list

- Offer a low-price webinar ($7 to $27) to a warm list, onboard buyers, over-deliver, then upsell a $1,000 program at the end. His math: 200 people, 50 sign up, 25 buy at $1,000. [Self-reported]
- Joint ventures: a partner mails their list (his example: 3,000), you co-teach a $7 two-hour session, and you split the $1,000 upsell revenue 50/50. He says these usually sell 30 to 50 seats. [Self-reported]

## Social media

- Keep politics off your accounts unless politics is your business. [Primary]
- Treat your accounts like a magazine: every post should match your brand. [Primary]
- Put the lead magnet on the front of every profile. [Primary]
- **Run a 30-post series** on your one core topic: short videos (30 to 60 seconds) or infographics, one a day. Each post offers the full series by email in exchange for name, email, and phone. The goal is to move followers onto a list you own, because platforms can lock you out. [Primary]
- Guzzi ranks YouTube highest for audience and LinkedIn highest for finding clients. [Primary]
- Personal posts work when they push your story forward, like a cause you support. Cut posts that don't, the way an editor cuts scenes that don't advance a movie. [Primary]

## Partnerships

Don't build your first product around other experts' ongoing participation. A one-off guest is fine. Build your own program first, and save promoter-style events with big names for when you have money to risk. [Primary]

Start events small. Get a free room, such as a pizza place that profits from 50 diners, instead of renting a theater you can't fill. Expect about 50 people at a first event. [Primary]

# Part 3: Prospecting, stage, media, and the speech

## LinkedIn prospecting (Ashley's session)

- Buy LinkedIn Sales Navigator (about $79 to $99 a month) and use its lead filters to build a list by title, company size, location, and function. A list of 5,000 to 6,000 is the sweet spot. [Primary]
- Visit each profile, follow the person, then send a connection request. Each step sends them a notification, so they see you three times before your message. [Primary]
- Write one message that fits about 80% of the list, plus follow-up messages a few days apart for people who connect without replying. Track them in a spreadsheet. [Primary]
- Ashley's tool automates these steps through the LinkedIn API. One campaign to coaches showed 38% connection acceptance and about 23% reply. Ashley puts average acceptance at 20%. [Self-reported]
- **Split-test messages:** after three to six weeks, cut the worst version, guess why the best one won, and write a new version that leans further into that trait. [Primary]
- Prospecting is a CEO-led method. Ashley says it scales to multiple six figures before you need a team, and credits it for Ashley's first half-million dollars. [Self-reported]

**Sideways selling rules:**

1. Be the most enthusiastic person in the conversation.
2. Be the most grateful person, and thank them for every small commitment.
3. Don't sell in the thread. Save the offer for the call.

Open with loaded questions that reveal their outlook. Move toward the call through small agreements: their industry, their situation, their challenge, and what the challenge costs them. Hold every call in your own Zoom room. [Primary]

Guzzi's variation: instead of booking calls, invite new LinkedIn connections to the two-hour seminar for free ("normally $100"). One two-hour seminar replaces dozens of sales calls. [Primary]

## Filling free talks

Pitch gyms, yoga studios, and other places with groups to host a free talk. Guzzi says three free talks fill about 30 seats, and speaking all three days of one conference can fill a room. [Self-reported]

## Running the room

- **Seat the laughers up front.** A comedy club manager taught Guzzi that every crowd has the same mix of good and bad audience members; the seating decides the night. [Primary]
- **Pivot to the good energy.** If the strong responders sit in the back, treat that area as the front, then pull the actual front in. [Primary]
- **Aim for four big laughs an hour** as a speaker. Start the next line when a laugh drops to about half volume. [Primary]
- **Repeat a word to anchor a laugh.** Words with hard K sounds work well. [Primary]
- **Use a ladder of heckler responses:** a soft "shh" into the mic, then a joke line, then a harder line only if needed. [Primary]
- **Name the elephant in the room**, including anything obvious about you. [Primary]
- **If nobody introduces you properly**, introduce yourself with an absurdly inflated bio. [Primary]
- **Manage negative energy.** In breakouts, pair difficult people with each other and pair fence-sitters with likely buyers. Head off attention-seekers before they reach the stage. [Primary]
- **Start strong and end strong.** Watch earlier speakers to learn what the crowd likes. [Primary]

## TV, radio, and podcasts

- A camera drains about half your energy, so go one step bigger on screen. [Primary]
- Local morning shows book guests often. Pitch the producer a human-interest angle tied to current events. [Primary]
- Pivot every question back to your agenda. Don't take the bait on politics or rivalries. [Primary]
- TV segments run about 5 to 6 minutes, radio about 20, and podcasts as long as the show. You only get paid as a booked expert commentator. [Primary]

**Bring a one-sheet** (printed, about 10 copies):

1. Your name in large type
2. Your name spelled phonetically
3. Your contact domain
4. Three softball questions for the host:
   - One that lets you describe the problem
   - One about why the problem hasn't been solved
   - One about the benefits, tied to the four motivations
5. Then pitch your unique selling proposition and end with a call to action: the lead magnet. A tagline is extra credit.

Answer in full paragraphs and keep talking until the host cuts in. [Primary]

## The nine-step free speech with an offer

Steps 1 to 3 (the engine) and 7 to 9 (the caboose) stay the same length every time. Steps 4 to 6 stretch or shrink to fit the time slot. Steps 7 to 9, the pitch, were covered in a later session that isn't in these notes. [Primary]

1. **Intro.** Use a 20 to 30 second recorded voiceover with music, listing only accolades relevant to the talk, with your name as the last two words. Walk on, take a power stance, pause, and ask three questions the audience will say yes to, each phrased differently.
2. **Table of contents.** Promise three things: your story, the myths, and three action items. Keep each one to a sentence or two.
3. **No questions.** Say the talk is part of a longer training, take no Q&A, and hand out a sheet with "questions" on one side and "aha moments" on the other. Point people to your back table. Guzzi says Q&A kills sales, especially "will you run this again?"
4. **Story.** Tell the full version: the mess, the turning point, and the successes (client wins work better than your own). Make the turning point match what you sell at the end, so you become their turning point. Aim to make them laugh and cry.
5. **Myths.** List 20 fears your audience has, turn them into myths, and keep the best seven. Dispel them without calling them myths.
6. **Three action items.** For example, a QR code for a free download. Save the strongest for last; for a hypnotist, that's a live demonstration.

Guzzi calls the paid version the "two-hour webinar" and the free version the "90-minute free speech with an offer" to keep them distinct. [Primary]

## The value proposition

- Build the offer so one result pays for it: one real estate listing, one booked gig. [Primary]
- A single bonus can close the sale. Guzzi bought a $1,000 class because it included a lawyer's legal documents he was about to pay for. [Self-reported]
- Amateur materials lose sales. One regular buyer skipped a $1,000 program because the PowerPoint looked cheap. [Self-reported]
- Guzzi says only three reasons stop a buyer: they don't need it, they won't use it, or they aren't the decision maker. Answer each during the talk. [Primary]

## Branding and positioning (Ashley's session)

Your brand covers everything a customer meets, not just the logo. Document these: [Primary]

- **Tonality:** the tones your brand uses, such as enthusiastic or direct
- **Brand vocabulary:** recurring phrases, even punctuation habits
- **Slogan:** optional, but useful on business cards and as a sign-off
- **Business description:** a full version, plus short versions sized for each social platform
- **Mission statement:** start from "I believe" statements, then add a motto and a community promise
- **Founder story:** the turning point that led you to start
- **Signature system:** name your offer ("breakthrough session") instead of listing a generic service with a price
- **Colors:** a primary, a secondary, and a contrasting action color for buttons. Ashley offers "action orange" (#FF8800) as a default.

Keep the look and language consistent from ad to landing page to member area. Ashley says a mismatch "smells wrong" to buyers. [Primary]

A core offer works best when it promises a change in status, such as going from stuck to confident. Work backward from the core offer to the tripwire and lead magnet. [Primary]

## Facebook ads (Joe's session)

- Define the demographic first: age, income, family stage, interests. Census data can show who has a given problem. [Primary]
- Set an end date; an ad left on "continuous" keeps spending. [Primary]
- In detailed targeting, add interests and acts similar to yours. [Primary]
- Carousel ads were performing well. [Primary]
- A run-of-show example: about $200 per show; one ad got 238 clicks at $0.72 each and sold out the show. [Self-reported]
- Ads outperform boosted posts, and business pages outperform personal profiles. [Primary]
- Invite people who liked an ad to like your page. [Primary]
- Plan a monthly content calendar and post on Facebook once or twice a day. [Primary]

## Ideas to use in this workshop

- Create a checkout link for the workshop, the order bump, and the main offer, including a payment-plan version. Save all of them in one note on your phone. (Step 3)
- Text the checkout link during every in-person conversation, then use the text thread as the follow-up list. (Steps 4 and 5)
- Put payment plans on automatic billing in the CRM. (Step 1)
- Open sales for the next workshop date before the current one ends. (Step 5)
- Put the lead magnet at the top of the registration site, and send lead magnet downloaders to the workshop. (Steps 3 and 4)
- Tag every funnel step separately so you can see where people drop off, and keep sales, onboarding, and completion sequences apart so the funnel can be reused. (Step 3)
- Write reminder emails backward from the workshop date, with a master welcome email for everyone. (Step 3)
- Run a 30-post social series on the workshop topic that trades the full series for an email address. (Step 4)
- Invite LinkedIn connections to the workshop instead of booking calls. (Step 4)
- Bring a one-sheet with three softball questions to every podcast or local TV spot. (Step 4)
- Structure the workshop pitch with the engine (intro, table of contents, no questions) and a story whose turning point matches the offer. (Step 5)
- Give the main offer a value proposition where one result pays for it, plus one bonus buyers would otherwise pay for. (Step 1)
